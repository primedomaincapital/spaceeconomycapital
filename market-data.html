The space economy crossed $600 billion in 2024, and most of that money never leaves the ground. The rockets get the photographs; the ground equipment gets the revenue.

Space Foundation's Space Report put the global space economy at $613 billion in 2024, up 7.8% year over year, with the commercial sector accounting for 78% of the total and government budgets the remaining 22%. Government space spending itself grew 6.7% to $132 billion, with the United States contributing $77 billion across national security and civil programs. The first half of the year also set a pace record, with 149 launches.

The satellite industry's own accounting tells the ground story. BryceTech's analysis for the Satellite Industry Association put global satellite industry revenue at $293 billion for 2024 — and $155.3 billion of it was ground equipment, ahead of $108.3 billion in satellite services, $20 billion in manufacturing, and just $9.3 billion in launch services. Launch is the visible tip; receivers, terminals, and ground networks are the business.

Launch prices show why the commercial share keeps growing. SpaceX's published Falcon 9 rate card moved from $67 million in 2022–2023 to $69.75 million in 2024 and $74 million in 2026, according to an archived-price table compiled from the company's rate cards. Those are sticker prices for a standard single launch, not negotiated mission prices — but the direction and the order of magnitude are what matter for anyone financing the sector.

Reading the numbers

The headline totals are not comparable without reading the definitions. Space Foundation's $613 billion for 2024 is a broad measure including commercial and government activity; the Satellite Industry Association's $293 billion counts satellite industry revenue only; the World Economic Forum and McKinsey $630 billion for 2023 includes space-enabled value beyond the sector itself. Joining them into one series would be a category error.

Launch prices are sticker prices. Multi-launch contracts, rideshare, and government missions are priced separately, and actual prices paid vary by mission — including whether the booster is recovered. The rate card is a floor for discussion, not a transaction record.

Methodology

Included: Space Foundation headline figures from its own press release, satellite industry revenue as summarized from BryceTech/SIA analysis, World Economic Forum and McKinsey estimates as summarized publicly, archived SpaceX rate-card prices as tabulated in a public paper, and NASA's FY 2025 budget as summarized by The Planetary Society. Excluded: paywalled Space Report detail, unpublished launch contract prices, and any investment-return figure for space equities or funds.
