Orbital Infrastructure: The Trillion-Dollar Bet

Satellites, spectrum, ground systems, and orbital compute are drawing record capital. What space investors must understand about infrastructure first.

Research · Prime Domain Capital

There is a useful thought experiment for understanding what changed in space investing: imagine telling a telecom analyst in 2015 that a single company's subscriber base would go from zero to ten million in six years, that its satellite internet service would post 63% EBITDA margins, and that it would then pay $17 billion for terrestrial spectrum so it could compete directly with the largest mobile carriers on Earth. They would have asked what you were smoking. In 2026, it is the S-1 filing.

Orbital infrastructure is the unglamorous term for the industry's most important development: space has become civilizational infrastructure — as load-bearing as fiber, as hard to replace as the power grid. Satellites now carry consumer broadband, military communications, GPS timing that financial markets depend on, Earth observation feeding insurance and agriculture, and, increasingly, the prospect of data centers themselves. The investors who understood railroads as infrastructure in the 1860s, or fiber as infrastructure in the 1990s, did better than the ones who fixated on individual operators. The same distinction applies in orbit.

What the Money Is Actually Building

Start with the constellation economics, because they are the proof of concept. SpaceX's Starlink generated roughly $11.4 billion in 2025 revenue, up 83%, with EBITDA margins near 63% — numbers that turn the old aerospace rulebook inside out. Traditional satellite operators ran multi-billion-dollar geostationary birds with 15-year lives and single-purpose payloads; the new model is mass-produced satellites in low Earth orbit, refreshed constantly, running software updates like a phone. By February 2026, Starlink counted more than 10 million active customers across 160 markets. The average revenue per user compresses as the service expands into emerging markets — roughly $120 a month in North America against ~$45 in parts of Latin America — but the response has been to go upmarket: aviation, maritime, and direct-to-cell services where pricing power is structurally stronger. Forty-two airlines now carry Starlink Wi-Fi.

The second build-out is the one most investors are underweighting: ground and spectrum infrastructure. The May 2026 approval of the ~$17 billion EchoStar spectrum acquisition gives orbital broadband a terrestrial-grade frequency portfolio, turning a satellite ISP into a potential direct carrier. This is the classic infrastructure play — acquire the scarce asset (spectrum licenses), layer it with owned capacity, and collect rents for decades. Whoever controls the spectrum controls the economics of the layer above it, regardless of whose satellites fly.

The third is defense and sovereign infrastructure, the demand floor nobody can dislodge. The Space Foundation measured the 2024 space economy at about $613 billion, with government spending roughly 22% — a share that behaves counter-cyclically, expanding when commercial budgets contract. In Q1 2026, space tech was the second-leading segment in defense-tech venture deal activity, with 40 deals worth about $2.7 billion. National constellations are proliferating: Europe, Japan, India, and several Gulf states all run procurement programs that function as long-dated, creditworthy customer contracts.

And then there is the speculative frontier: orbital compute. SpaceX's S-1 disclosed a $6.4 billion operating loss in its AI segment in 2025 and $7.7 billion of capital expenditure in the first quarter of 2026 alone. The pitch is thermodynamically seductive — constant solar flux, free radiative cooling, no land or grid constraints — and Google's "Project Suncatcher" roadmap targets solar-powered orbital data centers by 2027. The honest assessment: prototypes arrive no earlier than 2027, commercial scale is years beyond that, and even aggressive projections put orbital compute at single-digit shares of AI workloads before 2035. It is an option, not a cash flow. Price it accordingly.

Two adjacent layers deserve a mention before the skeptic's case. Space insurance and debris tracking have grown from afterthoughts into genuinely underwritten markets as constellation density rises — collision risk is now a priced input in business plans, not a footnote, and the firms modeling it sell to every operator in orbit. And the ground segment — antennas, gateways, mission-control software — quietly captures a durable share of every constellation's budget, often at better margins than the satellites themselves. Infrastructure, it turns out, is fractal: every layer of orbit has its own toll booths.

The Question Nobody at the Pitch Meeting Asks

Here is the line worth underlining: infrastructure gets paid last in the boom and first in the bust — which is exactly why you want to own it, at the right price. The trillion-dollar projections — $1 trillion by 2032 on the Space Foundation's math, $1.8 trillion by 2035 per the World Economic Forum and McKinsey, $2 trillion by 2040 per PwC — count vast economic activity enabled by space, not revenue flowing to infrastructure owners. A farmer using satellite data to cut fertilizer costs shows up in the $1.8 trillion. It shows up in John Deere's earnings, not a satellite operator's.

The investable version of the bet is narrower and more attractive than the headline: recurring-revenue connectivity with demonstrated margins, scarce spectrum and ground assets, contracted sovereign demand, and the supplier base that serves all three. That is the infrastructure stack. Everything above it — applications, compute, tourism, lunar logistics — is venture, and should be sized and priced like venture.

The trillion-dollar bet is real. Just make sure you know which trillion you're betting on.
