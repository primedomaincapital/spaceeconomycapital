Space SPACs to Space Funds: How Investing in Orbit Evolved

The 2021 space SPAC boom burned investors. The 2026 space market runs on venture funds, ETFs, and IPOs. How space investing grew up — and what changed.

Research · Prime Domain Capital

In February 2021, a prominent venture investor declared on television that space was "the next trillion-dollar industry" — and for about eighteen months, the public markets agreed with him on price, if not on timing. Between 2020 and 2022, more than a dozen space companies went public through SPAC mergers: Virgin Galactic had set the template back in 2019, and then came Rocket Lab, Planet Labs, AST SpaceMobile, Spire Global, Momentus, and a procession of others, each valued in the low single-digit billions at de-SPAC. The pitch decks were magnificent. The revenue was, in most cases, aspirational.

Then gravity did what gravity does. By 2023, the space SPAC cohort had become a case study in the difference between a technology thesis and a business. Most of the class traded 60–90% below their reference prices. Momentus burned through its capital and its launch contracts. Virgin Galactic spent years and billions on a vehicle that flew a handful of commercial missions. The SPAC structure itself deserved part of the blame — sponsors got paid to close deals, not to price them, and retail investors provided the exit liquidity. But the deeper failure was analytical: investors bought visions of orbital futures when they should have been underwriting launch manifests, backlog, and burn rates.

The damage was real and useful. Real because a generation of retail investors learned that "space" is not an investment thesis by itself. Useful because the wreckage cleared the field for a more serious capital structure.

The Institutional Turn

What replaced the SPAC era is more boring and much more durable. SpaceX's May 2026 S-1 — $18.7 billion in 2025 revenue, a profitable and fast-growing connectivity business inside it, and a public listing targeting roughly $1.75 trillion — is the canonical example: a company going public because the business is ready, not because a sponsor needed a deal. The IPO window for real space businesses is open again, and it is open on fundamentals.

The venture layer matured in parallel. Dedicated space funds — Space Capital's quarterly market reviews have become the industry's de facto scorecard, and firms like Seraphim Space, Airbus Ventures, and Embedded Ventures run specialist portfolios — replaced the generalist tourists. One 2026 tally put total space startup funding at a record $20.3 billion, with orbital compute the fastest-growing category. The defining feature of this vintage is selectivity: money flows to companies with contracted revenue or credible paths to it, and the bar for "credible" is materially higher than it was in 2021.

Public-market access democratized without the SPAC distortions. Space ETFs now let ordinary investors buy the theme with diversified exposure — and the flows are telling: in the month before May 23, 2026, space-related ETFs attracted roughly $1.3 billion of inflows, per Reuters, as investors positioned ahead of the SpaceX listing. The thematic vehicle works when it is a basket; the SPAC failed when it was a lottery ticket wearing a business suit.

Consolidation has accompanied the maturation. Well-capitalized operators are acquiring specialized capabilities — propulsion, terminals, ground software — in a pattern one 2026 industry review described as the sector entering its maturation phase. The definition of "a space company" now spans launch, data, and compute simultaneously, which is precisely why diversified exposure beats concentrated single-name bets for most investors allocating to the theme.

What the Second Act Requires

Three things separate this cycle from the last one, and investors should test every opportunity against them.

First, revenue first, story second. Starlink's 63% EBITDA margins and 10 million-plus subscribers are the standard against which new space businesses get measured. A credible 2026 space investment has a path to recurring revenue — communications, Earth observation sold as data subscriptions, defense contracts — not a path to "eventual monetization."

Second, cost structure discipline. The reason the current generation works is that launch costs fell more than 90% over the past decade. That deflation is now mostly banked, which means the next round of advantage comes from manufacturing scale and operational efficiency — satellite factories, not PowerPoints. Rocket Lab's cadence of Electron launches and its Neutron development program are instructive: the winners are the ones industrializing, not the ones rendering.

Third, honest timelines. Orbital data centers, lunar infrastructure, in-space manufacturing — all genuine markets, all measured in decades, not quarters. The S-1's own disclosure that the AI segment lost $6.4 billion in 2025 while spending $7.7 billion of capex in a single quarter of 2026 is the right level of honesty. Space rewards patient capital and punishes impatient capital with extreme prejudice.

Here is the quotable summary of the whole arc: the SPAC era tried to financial-engineer a space industry into existence; the 2026 era is financing one that already exists. The distinction is everything. One asked you to believe. The other asks you to read the income statement.

The industry grew up. The returns will accrue to the investors who noticed.
