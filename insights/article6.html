What Does It Cost to Launch a Satellite in 2026?

Research · Prime Domain Capital

On November 28, 2025, a single Falcon 9 lifted off from Vandenberg Space Force Base carrying 140 payloads for more than 30 customers in 16 countries. The manifest read like a trade show: Earth-imaging satellites, weather sensors, university CubeSats, a re-entry capsule. One booster carried them all, and the booster came back to land, refuel, and fly again. Fifteen years ago, most of those payloads would have waited years for a ride. In 2026, the question is no longer whether a small satellite can get to orbit. It is what the ride costs — and what that price does to the business riding on it.

The headline number is the one SpaceX publishes. A rideshare slot on a Transporter mission costs $350,000 for up to 50 kilograms to sun-synchronous orbit, with additional mass at $7,000 per kilogram. That is the price for 2026 missions, up from $325,000 for the same 50 kilograms when Transporter-15 flew in November 2025. The program began in August 2019 at $2.25 million for up to 150 kilograms — a price that looked like a provocation at the time and now reads as the opening bid in a market SpaceX has owned ever since.

A dedicated Falcon 9 is a different purchase. The list price is about $69.75 million for up to roughly 22.8 tonnes to low Earth orbit, which works out to near $3,000 per kilogram when the rocket flies full. Nobody flies it full except SpaceX itself. The comparison that matters is the historical one: sending a kilogram to orbit on the Space Shuttle cost roughly $54,000, in 2025 dollars. On a reused Falcon 9 it costs about $3,000. That is a fall of more than 90 percent in two decades, and it is the single fact behind every constellation business plan written in the last five years.

But the per-kilogram figure flatters the buyer who can fill the rocket and punishes the buyer who cannot. A rideshare slot has a $350,000 minimum whether the spacecraft weighs 5 kilograms or 50. A university team flying a 10-kilogram CubeSat pays the full entry fee, which makes its effective price $35,000 per kilogram — ten times the advertised rate. The published price is real. It is also a bulk price, and small satellites are by definition not bulk.

That gap is the whole reason dedicated small launchers still exist. Rocket Lab's Electron carries about 300 kilograms to a 500-kilometre sun-synchronous orbit for a published price of around $7.5 million per flight — roughly $25,000 per kilogram, more than triple the rideshare rate. What the customer buys for the premium is control: its own orbit, its own date, and no months of drift to reach the right orbital plane. Rocket Lab flew 21 Electron missions in 2025, up from 16 in 2024, and its launch backlog passed 100 missions in the autumn of 2026 after a 20-launch deal with the Japanese radar operator Synspective, signed on September 30. The economics are moving the right way for the company, too: average revenue per launch reached $9.2 million in the first half of 2026, against $7.5 million a year earlier, while cost per launch fell to $4.9 million from $5.3 million.

Firefly Aerospace's Alpha sits one size up: about 1,030 kilograms to low Earth orbit for roughly $15 million a flight, or about $14,563 per kilogram. The record is the problem. Alpha has flown seven times and fully succeeded three. A failure in April 2025 grounded the vehicle for about ten months before it returned to flight on March 12, 2026. A cheap dedicated ride that does not fly on schedule is not cheap, and insurers and constellation planners price that record into every contract.

Launch is no longer the hardest part of the space business. It is the part with a published price list — and the list is where the sorting happens.

Why the cheapest ride is not always the cheapest mission

Rideshare has hard edges that never appear in the price table. Transporter missions fly to a standard sun-synchronous orbit at roughly 500 to 600 kilometres. A satellite that needs a different inclination, a different altitude, or a specific orbital plane must spend its own propellant — and months of its commercial life — drifting there. For an Earth-imaging company whose revenue starts the day its satellite reaches its assigned plane, that drift is a cost as real as the launch invoice. For a communications constellation that needs dozens of planes filled on a schedule, it is a reason to buy the whole rocket.

Schedule is the second edge. A rideshare customer flies when the bus leaves. A dedicated customer flies when it is ready. Rocket Lab's pitch has always been responsiveness — a rocket in weeks, not a slot next year — and its military business proves the demand is real: in June 2026 the company set a benchmark for responsive space under the US Space Force's TacRS programme. The premium per kilogram buys time, and time is the one input a satellite operator cannot manufacture.

The third edge is the one investors should watch. SpaceX reportedly stopped accepting new rideshare bookings for flights departing after late 2028, according to trade publication SpaceQ in August 2026, as the company prepares to wind down Falcon 9. SpaceX has not confirmed the freeze publicly, and its storefront still lists rideshare slots. But the direction is plain: the vehicle that made small satellites affordable is not a permanent fixture. The market that grew up around its price list will have to find a new anchor.

The honest counterargument

The story that launch costs only fall is wrong in the detail that matters most. The advertised rideshare rate has crept up, not down: about $5,500 per kilogram in the early 2020s, $6,500 in 2023, $7,000 for 2026 missions. The ninety-percent fall is real measured against the Shuttle era, but the marginal price a small operator actually pays today is higher than it was four years ago — and it is set by one company with a manifest full into 2028.

Cheap launch also does not make a cheap mission. The satellite itself, the ground stations, the spectrum, the insurance, and the operations team still dominate the budget of most small-satellite ventures. A founder who budgets the launch at $350,000 and forgets the rest has mistaken the cheapest line item for the business. Several of the Earth-observation startups that rode Transporter missions to orbit in 2021 and 2022 learned this the expensive way: getting up was affordable; staying in business was not.

And the physics imposes a floor. A Falcon 9 carries about 17 tonnes in its reusable configuration, and its refurbishment cycle is already compressed to as little as 21 days. The per-kilogram cost of that vehicle is unlikely to fall much below $2,000 without a new rocket. The next step-change depends on vehicles that have not yet flown commercially at scale — Starship, Rocket Lab's Neutron, Relativity's Terran R, Blue Origin's New Glenn — and their promised prices are targets, not invoices.

What comes next

Neutron is the most concrete challenger. Rocket Lab's reusable medium-lift rocket is designed for about 13,000 kilograms, and the maths on the company's largest-ever contract — five Neutron and three Electron launches for one undisclosed customer, signed in May 2026 — implies a Neutron price near $44 million per flight. That would put it clearly below the rideshare line on a per-kilogram basis for the first time: a medium rocket that competes with the bus on price while offering a dedicated schedule.

Relativity Space is building Terran R, a reusable rocket rated at 23.5 tonnes, after an investment of about $800 million from former Google chief executive Eric Schmidt in late 2025. Blue Origin's New Glenn flew into the market in 2026. Stoke Space, backed by more than $510 million, is developing the fully reusable Nova for a first flight in 2026. None of them has published a price. All of them are aimed at the same gap: the space between a $7.5 million small rocket and a $70 million Falcon 9, where a constellation operator wants a full rocket at a rideshare price.

What it means for capital

For an investor underwriting a space business in 2026, launch is a filter with three questions. How much mass does the business need in orbit? How precisely must that mass be placed? And how much does schedule slippage cost? A company that answers "a lot, anywhere, whenever" should be on a rideshare at $7,000 a kilogram. A company that answers "a little, exactly here, next month" should be paying Rocket Lab $25,000 a kilogram and calling it cheap. The businesses that fail are the ones that buy the wrong answer — a dedicated rocket for a payload that could have ridden the bus, or a rideshare slot for a satellite whose revenue depends on an orbit the bus will never reach.

The repricing to watch is not in small launch. It is in medium lift. If Neutron or Terran R flies at its target price, the rideshare premium collapses and the dedicated-launch market reorganises around whoever can fill a 13-tonne rocket every month. If they slip — and new rockets usually slip — SpaceX keeps the price-setting power, and the $7,000-a-kilogram bus stays the reference price for the whole industry. Either way, the era when launch was the binding constraint is over. The binding constraint is now everything that happens after the satellite separates.
