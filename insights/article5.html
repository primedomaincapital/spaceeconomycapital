Golden Dome: The $38 Billion Order Book Reshaping Commercial Space

Research · Prime Domain Capital

On December 19, 2025, the Space Development Agency awarded $3.5 billion for 72 missile-tracking satellites, split four ways between Lockheed Martin, L3Harris Technologies, Rocket Lab USA, and Northrop Grumman — 18 satellites each, under the Tranche 3 Tracking Layer. Rocket Lab, a company that started as a launch startup, took $805 million as a prime contractor. The defense space market has new owners, and the award notice said so more plainly than any strategy document could.

The money behind that award arrived fast. President Trump signed the One Big Beautiful Bill into law on July 4, 2025, with a $25 billion initial investment in the Golden Dome missile defense initiative. Congress subsequently passed the fiscal 2026 defense appropriations bill on February 3, 2026, adding $13.4 billion in space and missile defense systems for Golden Dome-related programs. Combined, that is more than $38 billion committed to a single program in the span of eight months — the largest sustained defense space investment in a generation. New Space Economy’s analysis of the Golden Dome opportunity traces the two appropriations and the procurement cycle they opened.

The total cost depends on who is counting, and the spread is the story. The Pentagon’s current estimate puts Golden Dome at about $185 billion to deploy its planned objective architecture through 2035 — up from $175 billion after program head General Michael Guetlein told reporters in March 2026 that the office had been asked to procure additional space capabilities. The Congressional Budget Office’s September 2026 testimony models a notional national missile-defense system at about $1.191 trillion over 20 years, with roughly $743 billion of that for the space-based interceptor constellation alone — some 7,800 satellites in low-Earth orbit. The nonprofit Taxpayers for Common Sense projects full program costs at $3.6 trillion over 20 years. The Pricer’s October 2026 cost review lays out the three estimates and what each one actually prices.

The buying vehicle is as important as the budget. The Missile Defense Agency’s SHIELD contract — Scalable Homeland Innovative Enterprise Layered Defense — is a ten-year vehicle worth up to $151 billion, and it approved 2,440 of 2,463 applicants by mid-January 2026. The pool runs from primes such as Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman, and Raytheon alongside venture-backed firms like Anduril Industries and Palantir, launch providers such as SpaceX and Rocket Lab. The Tranche 3 split shows the new shape of the market: Lockheed Martin at $1.1 billion, L3Harris at $843 million, Rocket Lab at $805 million, and Northrop Grumman at $764 million. A second, accelerated award followed in July 2026 — $1.75 billion for 36 infrared missile-tracking satellites, split between L3Harris at up to $955 million and Sierra Space at up to $798 million, with all 36 spacecraft expected ready for launch by the end of 2028. Weaponspecs’ report on the July 2026 award records the split and the 2028 readiness target; New Space Economy’s military space market analysis details the SHIELD pool and the Tranche 3 awards.

The new entrants are not waiting for permission. Space-based interceptor prototypes went to Anduril, Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman, and True Anomaly in November 2025 under classified Other Transaction Agreements. Firefly Aerospace acquired SciTec for $855 million to build a full-stack position from launch through ground processing. Northwood Space raised $100 million and won a $49 million Space Force contract. Northrop Grumman’s manifest across Tranches 1 through 3 now totals 150 satellites. The incumbents still dominate the dollars — SpaceX holds approximately $7.2 billion in Defense Department obligations against more than $1.4 trillion held by the four legacy primes — but the direction of travel is toward proliferated constellations built by whoever can manufacture at volume.

Golden Dome is not a program with a space component. It is a space program with a missile-defense name — and it is buying like one.

The honest counterargument

As of early 2026, no hardware had been deployed, Congress had not received a master deployment schedule, cost estimate, performance metrics, or finalized system architecture, and the Office of Management and Budget was reported to be holding up $14 billion in approved spending. The $185 billion versus $1.19 trillion versus $3.6 trillion spread does not describe three views of one program; it describes three different programs, and the Pentagon has not said which one it is building. The interceptor layer sits in prototype development with a 2028 demonstration target that independent analysts view skeptically on both technical and strategic grounds.

That uncertainty is a real risk for anyone treating Golden Dome as a guaranteed order book. Appropriations are not contracts, contracts are not revenue, and a program whose architecture is still classified can be rescoped by a single budget cycle. The firms that win here will be the ones whose businesses survive a delay, not the ones whose pitch decks assume the $185 billion arrives on schedule.

What it means for capital

The proliferated-constellation model is now the funded model — recurring satellite orders, not one-off exquisite systems. The winners are firms that can manufacture at volume: Rocket Lab’s debut as a missile-defense prime is the template, and the Tranche 3 split shows the government is willing to pay new primes at the same table as the old ones. The risk is appropriation timing, not demand. For investors in the space economy, Golden Dome has already answered the only question that mattered: whether the largest customer in the market would buy commercial space the way commercial space builds it. The $3.5 billion award in December said yes.
